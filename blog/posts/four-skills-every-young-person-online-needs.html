Digital Netizenship

The four skills every young person online actually needs

7 min read · By Hamcodes

Most online safety material fails in the same way. It hands a child a list of twenty rules, the child nods, and nothing about their behaviour changes. The list is too long to remember and too abstract to use at the moment it matters, which is always a moment of hurry or excitement.

Four skills do most of the work. Each one is teachable in a single sitting. Each one shows up in a real situation a young person will meet this year.

One: recognising a phish

A phishing message works by creating urgency and borrowing trust. It looks like it came from somewhere familiar, and it wants a decision right now. Those two things together are the tell, and they are the tell whether the message arrives by email, by text, or in a game chat.

The teachable version is not a list of warning signs. It is a habit: when a message wants something from you quickly, slow down and check the sender by a route the message did not give you. Open the app yourself. Type the address yourself. Ask the person on a different channel.

A child who has taken one phishing email apart, line by line, stops falling for the whole category. The trick only works while it is invisible.

The worksheet for this one puts six realistic messages in front of a student and asks them to mark what gives each one away. It works better than any warning, because the student finds the answer rather than being handed it.

Two: protecting an account

Passwords are taught badly almost everywhere. Children are told to use symbols and numbers, which produces short, complicated passwords that are easy for a computer to guess and hard for a human to remember. Length is what matters. Four ordinary words in a row beats a short string of punctuation, and a student will actually be able to type it twice.

The second half of this skill is two-factor authentication, explained without jargon: a second lock that lives on something you hold. Turn it on for the account the student cares most about, which at that age is usually a game or a social account, and the idea sticks.

Three: understanding a footprint

This is the one that lands hardest, and it needs no lecture at all. Have students search for themselves. Have them search for a parent. Have them look at what a public profile shows to someone who is not a friend.

The point is not to frighten anyone. It is to replace a vague sense that the internet remembers things with a specific picture of what it already has. Once a student has seen their own results, a conversation about what they post next becomes concrete instead of theoretical.

Four: noticing where attention goes

Screen time gets framed as a discipline problem, which puts the adult and the child on opposite sides immediately. It works better framed as a design problem: these apps are built by people whose job is to keep you scrolling, and they are good at it. That is not a moral failing on the student's part, it is an engineering achievement on the company's part.

From there the exercise is simple. One honest week of numbers, then one question: which of these hours would you take back if you could? Students answer that question more honestly than adults expect, as long as nobody is being told off.

Why four and not twenty

A short list gets remembered. Each of these four skills covers a whole category of risk rather than a single scenario, so a student who has them can reason about a situation nobody prepared them for. That is the actual goal. Not compliance with a list, but the ability to think clearly at the moment something feels off.

Each skill has a ten-minute story game on the Netizen site and a printable worksheet in the free workbook. Run the game first, hand out the sheet after, and you have a class period that changes something.
